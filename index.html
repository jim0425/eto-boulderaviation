Why we don't use the same technology as Picarro and CleanAir.

Boulder iQ uses purpose-built ethylene oxide detection technology that costs less because it does less — exactly what your facility needs, and nothing it doesn't.

What Picarro and CleanAir actually sell you

Picarro and CleanAir Engineering both use cavity ring-down spectroscopy (CRDS) — a precision laser-absorption analyzer originally developed for atmospheric science research. The same Picarro CRDS analyzer family that monitors ethylene oxide in your sterilization facility also measures methane in arctic ice cores, water isotopes for wine fraud detection, formaldehyde in semiconductor cleanrooms, and trace gases for industrial leak surveys. It is a remarkable scientific instrument. CleanAir's MET-44 is built on the same Picarro analyzer module — that's per CleanAir's own marketing.

It is also extraordinarily expensive. Analyzer hardware alone runs five to ten times the cost of purpose-built EtO detection. That cost shows up in every quote those vendors send you, regardless of whether your facility actually needs research-grade sensitivity for molecules you don't measure.

What Boulder iQ uses instead

Boulder iQ uses purpose-built ethylene oxide detection technology that has been in continuous industrial use for decades. It does one thing exceptionally well: continuously measure ethylene oxide in workplace air at the parts-per-billion sensitivity required by FIFRA and OSHA, with the reliability and lifecycle costs of a maintenance-friendly industrial instrument.

It does not measure carbon dioxide, methane, water isotopes, formaldehyde, or any of the other gases CRDS can detect. Your sterilization facility does not need a multi-gas research analyzer to comply with EtO regulations — it needs an EtO instrument that works reliably, calibrates simply, and produces defensible regulatory data. Frequently asked questions.

Will this work with my 3M Steri-Vac, Solventum, or Anderson chambers?
Yes. Boulder iQ was designed specifically around combination chambers (sterilize and aerate in the same vessel) which is the architecture used by 3M Steri-Vac, the Solventum-rebranded successor models, and Anderson sterilizers. Combination units actually simplify NESHAP compliance because they eliminate two separate emission points (the Aeration Room Vent and Chamber Exhaust Vent) that other facility designs require.
What's the difference between parametric monitoring and CEMS, and why does it matter?
Continuous Emission Monitoring (CEMS) requires a PS-19 certified analyzer continuously sampling the exhaust stack. Parametric monitoring tracks the operating conditions of your control device (temperature, flow, etc.) plus periodic performance tests — the EPA explicitly allows this approach under NESHAP. Parametric is significantly cheaper. CleanAir doesn't sell parametric, so their customers default to CEMS at $717,000 even when they don't need it. Boulder iQ supports both.
What costs are NOT included in the Picarro and CleanAir invoice prices?
Several. Both vendors push the following costs off-invoice to the customer: calibration gas (zero gas plus 5 ppm EtO/N2 specialty span gas, ~$10K-12K/year recurring), sample tubing installation labor ($12K-25K one-time depending on point count), electrical and IT setup ($3K-6K one-time), and most importantly compliance reporting consulting if you outsource it (~$15K/year for routine NESHAP semiannual reports, annual certifications, and CEDRI submittal preparation). Picarro additionally requires you to buy your own calibration gas regulators (~$600). CleanAir does not bundle sample tubing material (~$4K extra). Boulder iQ is the only vendor that bundles compliance reporting and submittal in the price — saving $75,000 over five years versus retaining a regulatory consultant separately. What does each vendor's "reporting" actually include?
Picarro Annual Service Plan ($50K/yr) includes "custom dashboards, reports, and alarms" — meaning configurable dashboards and on-demand reports generated from the analyzer's data. It does NOT include preparation of the formal NESHAP semiannual or annual compliance reports, nor electronic submittal through CEDRI. CleanAir Essential ($3K/mo) includes quarterly summary reports and a site-specific QA framework. CleanAir Enhanced (more expensive tier) adds monthly internal compliance reviews. CleanAir Exclusive (most expensive tier) adds weekly QA documentation. None of those tiers include preparation and electronic filing of the §63.367(c) semiannual report or §63.367(d) annual certification — customers DIY or hire a consultant. Boulder iQ auto-generates both the semiannual and annual reports from your monitoring data and prepares the CEDRI submittal package in our standard pricing. The reports actually get filed for you.
When do I need to be compliant?
FIFRA continuous indoor monitoring takes effect via amended pesticide product labels — most facilities should plan for January 2027. NESHAP compliance demonstration is due October 5, 2027 for existing area sources; Notification of Compliance Status due December 6, 2027. Boulder iQ ships within 12 weeks of order acceptance, so you can start any time and still beat both deadlines.
What about the 2024 NESHAP rule reconsideration?
The EPA proposed reconsidering parts of the 2024 Final Rule on March 17, 2026 (91 FR 12700). Final action is expected late 2026 or 2027. The reconsideration adds compliance flexibility, particularly for the parametric monitoring path — which favors Boulder iQ's recommended bundle. FIFRA worker exposure limits are completely unaffected by this NESHAP action; those drive indoor monitoring requirements regardless of how the NESHAP reconsideration finalizes.

What sensitivity does your analyzer measure?
Boulder iQ measures down to 50 parts per billion (0.05 ppm) — that's 1/5 of the 2031 worker exposure limit (0.25 ppm = 250 ppb) and below the 2035 limit of 100 ppb. Our analyzer cycles 12 sample points with a 5-minute measurement per point. For high-exposure locations needing faster coverage of OSHA's 15-minute STEL, we offer optional dedicated continuous point sensors.
